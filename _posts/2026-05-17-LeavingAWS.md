## Why I Left AWS (A Clearer Way to Explain a Complicated Choice)

I get asked about this a lot.  
More than feels proportional.

Despite spending *years* in other industries and roles, people keep circling back to why I left **AWS**—a comparatively short chapter in my career.

Lately, I’ve realized the fatigue doesn’t come from the question itself.  
It comes from the fact that the answer **can’t be reduced to a sound bite**. Answering it feels like the ramblings of a madman.

There was no single catalyst.  
No dramatic fallout.  
No villain-of-the-week.

It was a layered, compounding experience that started early and quietly got worse.

This is the clearest version I can give—once—so I can stop rambling through it every time.

---

## The Problems Started Almost Immediately

### 1. Onboarding, Mentorship, and Leadership (Before Anything Else)

Before culture, before layoffs, before return-to-office—there was onboarding.

And it was rough.

My onboarding and mentoring experience was deeply inconsistent:
- Difficulty getting time with my assigned mentor  
- Little structured guidance  
- Constant friction getting answers I genuinely needed  

What made it worse was watching peers have **excellent mentors**:
- Regular touchpoints  
- Advocacy  
- Clear guidance  

Those peers succeeded faster. That wasn’t subjective—it was obvious.

Mentorship mattered. I didn’t have it.

This early instability set the tone. I never felt fully grounded, and that’s not something you can easily recover from in a high-pressure environment.

So what did I do to address this?
- Frequently made mention of my concerns to management
- Attempted multiple pathways to communicate with OB and Mentor
- Escalated to higher leadership 

---

### 2. The Role I Didn’t Want (But Got Anyway)

When I was hired, I was offered **three potential role paths**.

I was placed into:
- My **last choice**
- The least aligned with my long-term goals
- The furthest from hands-on cybersecurity work

I tried to make it work. But starting misaligned—while lacking strong mentorship—made progress feel uphill from day one.

I never gave up on my work, however, I knew there were countless opportunities and if I had to make my way through the mailroom - that worked just fine for me but it would play an important part when...

---

## Then the Company I Joined Started Disappearing

### 3. “We’re Proudly Remote”… Until We Weren’t

I was hired fully remote into a company that loudly celebrated being remote-first.

Shortly after:
- Spending was frozen  
- Travel budgets vanished  
- Annual company gatherings were canceled 
- Return to office rumors 

I never attended **a single in-person AWS event**. I never met a single person face to face. That chapter in my life feels like a fever dream. Did it really happen?

Culture doesn’t survive on internal tools alone.  
That early pullback killed morale before it ever had a chance to grow.


---

### 4. Hiring Freezes and the Slow Stall

Then came the hiring freezes.

Not temporary pauses—**momentum killers**:
- Teams stretched thin  
- Work redistributed  
- Growth quietly deprioritized  

When hiring eventually resumed, it wasn’t the same environment anymore.

Because... 

---

### 5. Return to Office (And the End of Mobility)

I was based in Colorado.  
My “home office” was Portland.

There was no nearby office.

Once return-to-office became real:
- Remote promotion paths vanished  
- Lateral movement dried up  
- “Be remote *and* advance” stopped being an option  

The ladder didn’t get steeper.  
It got removed.

This amplified the dread of the daily grind and the feeling that... 

---

## The Culture Shift Wasn’t Subtle

### 6. Metrics Over Humanity

AWS runs on metrics. Metrics are fine.

**Metric absolutism is not.**

It felt like:
- If you weren’t a metric superstar, you weren’t allowed to be human  
- Personal lives were tolerated only for senior, established high performers  
- Everyone else was expected to stay quiet and produce  

Efficient? Yes.  
Human? No.

But I still have my work, and I can learn so much, there was stability until... 

---

### 7. Layoffs—Public, Massive, and Constant

In my short time there:
- **Three public layoffs**
- Each one larger than the last
- Each one involving thousands of people  

Every workday carried background noise:
> *Is today the day?*

That kind of stress doesn’t spike—it erodes.

That couldn't possibly trigger human response to defend their own resources, right?
It couldn't possibly lead to...

---

### 8. Tribalism and Isolation After RTO

Once return-to-office was fully committed:
- Local-office staff clustered together  
- Senior engineers protected their circles  
- Escalations became harder  
- Collaboration became political  

If you weren’t physically present, you felt it.

Isolation doesn’t need hostility to work.

BUT I still had my work and that work - what's the worst that could happen? 
I can deal with all of this.
Until I discovered the final problem...

---

## The Breaking Point Was Meaning

### 9. I Didn’t Believe in What the Work Was For

This part took longer to admit—to myself.

I struggled with the idea that my work ultimately existed to:
- Generate revenue  
- Optimize profit  
- Make already enormous systems even more money  

I searched for something—*anything*—that felt like a greater good.

I came up empty.

One moment crystallized it:
I was once **yelled at by an engineer whose company sold online content - which in no way would be considered beneficial to society.**.

That’s not a metaphor.  
That’s not a joke.

It’s an awful feeling to realize:
> *This is what my stress is serving.*

So... 
---

## Why I Left—and Where I Went Instead

### 10. Choosing Cybersecurity (Again) — For the Right Reasons

That clarity pushed me to make a decision.

I wanted back into a pure cybersecurity role:
- Where people actually wanted my opinion  
- Where judgment mattered more than dashboards  
- Where the work protected people, systems, and public trust  

I wanted to contribute to something that felt **constructive**, not extractive.

I wanted purpose.  
And I wanted my family back at the dinner table.

By the time the layoff finally came, I had already mentally left months earlier.

---

## The Short Version

I didn’t leave because AWS is “bad.”

I left because:
- The company I joined no longer existed  
- Support systems failed early  
- Growth paths closed  
- Culture shifted toward metrics and fear  
- The work lost meaning  
- And my family paid the price  

---

## Final Thought

AWS was a chapter—not the book.

I learned things I couldn’t have learned elsewhere.  
I met brilliant people.  
I gained perspective that still shapes how I work.

But staying would have required becoming someone I didn’t recognize.

And that’s not a trade I’m willing to make.

If you’re still curious after this—feel free to ask.  
This is the version I’ll be pointing to from now on.
