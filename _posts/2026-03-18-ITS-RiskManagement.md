## When Risk Has a 15-Year Service Life

In traditional IT, “end of life” often means *next quarter*.  
In **Intelligent Transportation Systems (ITS)**, end of life means *after the asphalt gives up first*.

Roadside devices don’t live in climate-controlled data centers. They live on poles, in cabinets, under bridges, and in environments where **funding cycles are short, hardware lifespans are long, and replacement is rare**. That reality fundamentally changes how risk management must work.

The risk management lifecycle in ITS isn’t about chasing perfection.  
It’s about building something **durable, visible, and survivable**—even when vendors, products, and contracts disappear.

---

## The ITS Risk Management Lifecycle (Reality Edition)

Most risk frameworks look clean on paper. ITS risk management does not.  
Here’s how the lifecycle *actually* plays out when devices are expected to remain roadside for a decade or more.

### 1. Identify Risk (With Longevity in Mind)

Risk identification in ITS starts with the obvious:
- Legacy operating systems
- Embedded firmware
- Limited patch windows
- Physical exposure

But the *real* question is this:

> **Will this risk still be manageable when the vendor no longer exists or the product is no longer supported?**

If mitigation depends entirely on a manufacturer portal, subscription, or cloud service, that risk has an expiration date—and it’s usually **shorter than the device’s deployment life**.

---

### 2. Analyze Risk (Funding Is a Threat Actor)

In government environments, funding constraints are not hypothetical. They are predictable.

Risk analysis must assume:
- Delayed refresh cycles
- Deferred upgrades
- Staggered deployments across years
- Mixed firmware and hardware generations

This shifts analysis away from “best possible control” toward **best sustainable control**.

A compensating control that can be **operated internally for 15 years** is often lower risk than a perfect vendor provided control that expires in year three.

---

### 3. Treat Risk (Compensating Controls Win Over Time)

This is where ITS diverges from enterprise IT.

Vendor-managed security controls:
- Require renewals
- Depend on continued product support
- Assume stable budgets
- Assume vendor longevity

Internal compensating controls:
- Are visible
- Are auditable
- Can be trained, documented, and transferred
- Survive procurement changes

In long-lived roadside environments, **network segmentation, traffic monitoring, access restrictions, and logging** consistently outlast vendor promises.

The less a mitigation depends on an external roadmap, the longer it remains effective.

---

### 4. Monitor Risk (Because Nothing Stays Static)

ITS environments change slowly—but they *do* change:
- Network paths evolve
- Central systems are modernized
- Threats adapt to exposed infrastructure

Continuous monitoring doesn’t mean bleeding-edge tools. It means:
- Consistent telemetry
- Clear ownership
- Alerts people understand
- Logs someone actually reviews

If monitoring can’t be maintained during staff turnover, budget cuts, or reorganization, it’s not durable enough.

---

### 5. Review and Adjust (Without Replacing the Device)

Risk reviews in ITS are rarely followed by immediate remediation.  
They’re followed by prioritization.

The lifecycle must allow for:
- Deferred treatment
- Layered mitigation
- Incremental improvement

The goal is not to “close findings.”  
The goal is to **prevent risk from growing silently** over the lifespan of the asset.

---

## Why Internal Controls Age Better Than Vendor Controls

Vendors build products for market cycles.  
Government builds infrastructure for decades.

That mismatch matters.

Internal compensating controls:
- Don’t get end-of-lifed
- Don’t require license renewals
- Don’t disappear after acquisitions
- Don’t assume always-on connectivity

This aligns naturally with established frameworks like :contentReference[oaicite:0]{index=0} risk management guidance—but with a practical, infrastructure-first interpretation rather than a cloud-first one.

---

## Designing Risk for the Long Road Ahead

Effective ITS risk management accepts uncomfortable truths:
- Some devices will never be patched
- Some vendors will vanish
- Some risks will be accepted—intentionally

Durability comes from **visibility, ownership, and control**.

If a mitigation can be explained to a new engineer ten years from now—and still works—it belongs in the lifecycle.

Everything else is temporary.

---

*In ITS, risk management isn’t about keeping up.*  
*It’s about holding steady—long after the purchase order is forgotten.*
